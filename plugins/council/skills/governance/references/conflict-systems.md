# Conflict systems for small co-ops

Use when discussing disagreements, reporting harm, mediation, appeals,
facilitator capacity, or community accountability. A conflict process is a
human practice supported by tools; it is not simply a vote or moderation queue.

## What the book contributes

- **Prepare before a crisis:** clarify expectations, responsible people,
  possible consequences, and opportunities for repair or appeal (pp.18–19,
  30–31). Rules need not be elaborate to be useful.
- **Make space for conflict:** move sensitive dialogue out of performative
  public channels; support slower, facilitated conversation (pp.9, 18–19).
- **Recognize limits:** some matters need outside facilitation or another
  appropriate source of help (pp.17, 19, 25).
- **Fit the community:** size, timing, culture, norms, and surrounding
  institutions change what a fair process looks like (pp.22–25).
- **Support the people doing the work:** facilitation, training, time, and
  emotional sustainability matter as much as software (pp.12–13, 19, 32–33).
- **Build capacity:** tools should help people learn to handle disagreement,
  rather than make them dependent on a system (p.28).

The Small Hassles Court example deliberately addresses minor disagreements
between people of equal power. Understanding, a workable boundary, or an
agreed separation can be useful outcomes even without agreement (pp.6–7).
That example is not a protocol for abuse or serious harm.

## Council application: choose a proportionate response

The following is our application of those principles to small worker co-ops,
not a prescribed ladder from the book or a statement of statutory procedure.

| Situation | Start by establishing | Possible support |
|---|---|---|
| Routine disagreement over a proposal | Decision owner, interests, adopted process, unresolved objections | Clarification, revised proposal, facilitated discussion |
| Interpersonal misunderstanding | Willingness to talk, suitable setting, power differences, desired outcome | Voluntary conversation or a mutually acceptable facilitator |
| Report of harm, coercion, discrimination, or retaliation | Immediate support needs, safe contact route, applicable policy, independent capacity | A qualified person or external process suited to the situation |
| Complaint about an administrator, director, or facilitator | Who can act without the implicated person's control | Recusal, an alternative contact, outside support if local independence is unavailable |
| Cross-co-op conflict | Which co-ops are affected and what each has agreed to handle | Named human contacts and an agreed referral; no automatic cross-instance authority |

Do not require direct confrontation, mediation, reconciliation, forgiveness, or
exhaustion of informal steps as a condition of receiving support. Do not treat
an allegation as an established finding. Where safety or applicable legal duties
are implicated, obtain context-appropriate qualified support rather than applying
a generic dialogue template. Verify current jurisdiction-specific requirements
before advising on employment, member removal, reporting, or legal remedies.

## A minimal agreement, not an enterprise case-management system

For a current co-op need, help participants answer:

1. **How do I get help?** Name an accessible private contact and an alternative
   if that person is involved or unavailable. Offer a non-digital route too.
2. **What happens next?** Explain acknowledgment, who handles the matter, what
   information is needed, and when to expect another update. A response target
   is not a promise of a resolution date.
3. **Who can see what?** Separate process transparency from personal accounts.
   Agree access, confidentiality limits, and necessary records; do not promise
   absolute secrecy or put case details in a public proposal or routine audit payload.
4. **Who has authority?** Separate facilitation, support, findings, and decisions.
   Identify conflicts of interest and any agreed reconsideration or appeal route.
5. **What outcomes are possible?** Clarification, repair, changed practices,
   boundaries, an authorized consequence, or referral may all be relevant.
   An administrative closure does not prove relationships were repaired.
6. **Can we sustain this?** Identify available time, training, cover for absence,
   and when the group must seek outside help. Revisit the arrangement after use.

A contact, a short policy, and an agreed meeting may meet the need. Start with
those before proposing a Fixture, queue, state machine, or escalation service.
Use a document or existing feature when it is adequate; build only an observed
missing capability. Keep community practices adaptable rather than universal.

## Application to Assembly design and review

These checks apply only when the affected feature handles conflict, delegated
authority, member consequences, or associated communications:

- Can a member understand who is responsible and what the next step is?
- Can someone seek help when the ordinary contact is the subject of the concern?
- Are private accounts kept separate from information the wider membership
  needs about the process or a resulting policy change?
- Can participants pause, ask for clarification, or access the agreed review
  route without being represented as obstructive or automatically consenting?
- Are notification frequency, accessibility, translation needs, and facilitator
  workload appropriate to this actual community?
- Is any consequence grounded in the adopted process and an authorized human
  decision, rather than a technical administrator's convenience?

Treat these as questions tied to actual scope and requirements, not a mandate
to invent new P1–P3 findings or add all capabilities to every Fixture. Preserve
the prototype's established UI and interactions; propose deliberate changes
through the owning product process rather than silently redesigning it.

The Common showcase suggests separating a complex process into smaller
decisions while recognizing that deliberation spans stages (pp.26–27). This
supports clearer explanations; it does not require a generic workflow engine.
Beacon's maintenance difficulties (pp.12–13) reinforce the need for a small,
maintainable implementation. Kleros's blockchain and jury mechanisms (pp.20–21)
are examples, not requirements for Assembly. Federation does not authorize
sharing private conflict reports across co-ops.

## AI assistance and limits

AI may help explain an adopted policy, prepare questions, or draft a summary
for participants to correct. Do not use a model, Jev score, or sentiment label
to determine credibility, blame, sanctions, member removal, or whether repair
is complete. Keep the underlying accounts and uncertainties distinguishable.
The book warns about automated systems bypassing care and context (p.17) and
emphasizes learning and human capacity (p.28); this is Council's practical
boundary derived from those concerns.

## Review walkthroughs

Use these as human-readable acceptance scenarios, not claimed automated proof:

| Scenario | Expected Council guidance |
|---|---|
| Six members disagree about the rota | Check existing delegation and use a short conversation; do not invent a tribunal or default voting threshold. |
| A member reports the usual contact | Offer a route outside that person's control; no compulsory confrontation. |
| A volunteer facilitator is overloaded | Set realistic updates and arrange support or referral; do not promise instant resolution. |
| A public proposal includes sensitive allegations | Separate policy deliberation from access-limited personal accounts; no blanket secrecy claim. |
| A tool marks a case closed | Distinguish administrative status from agreement, safety, repair, and human follow-up. |
| Two federated co-ops disagree | Establish their agreed authority and contacts; no automatic report replication or cross-co-op sanctions. |
