# Designing community governance

Use for co-op discovery, delegation, operating agreements, and reviewing who
can make or challenge a decision. This is organizational design guidance, not
a statutory reference or a request to implement every governance model.

## Make actual authority understandable

The booklet argues that informal groups still develop power structures;
making authority explicit lets participants understand and challenge it.
Rules support relationships but cannot substitute for them (pp.4–7).

In discovery, distinguish the co-op's present arrangements from its desired
ones. Ask who actually decides, including the founder, the person holding
passwords, and the person doing unpaid coordination. Do not assume that a
friendly group has no power imbalances, or that a formal hierarchy is the only
way to make responsibility clear.

For each recurring decision, capture a short answer to:

- What is the decision, and which person or body owns it?
- Who is affected, who must be consulted, and who can initiate a proposal?
- What authority and limits have been delegated? Who can revise or withdraw them?
- Which decision process applies, and what happens when agreement is absent?
- Who implements the outcome, tells affected people, and checks whether it worked?

For a small co-op, a page of agreed responsibilities may be sufficient. This
does not imply a permissions redesign, policy engine, new committee, or service.
Distinguish operational responsibility from authority reserved to members or
directors under the co-op's verified rules and applicable law.

## Compare arrangements before prescribing one

The booklet offers starting points to combine or adapt, not a universal optimum
(pp.4, 7). The following are Council's practical questions about those patterns.

| Arrangement | Source | Useful question for a small co-op |
|---|---|---|
| Founder-led authority | p.10 | What power is concentrated, how is it accountable, and what ends the temporary arrangement? |
| Circles with delegated domains | p.14 | Can each group act within a clear domain, and where do overlapping responsibilities get resolved? |
| Consensus | p.18 | How are serious objections heard and addressed, and who carries out an agreed proposal? |
| Initiative-led work (do-ocracy) | p.22 | Which reversible tasks can people undertake, and when do effects on others require consultation? |
| Elected board | p.26 | What mandate is delegated, and how do members select, review, and replace representatives? |
| Randomly selected deliberative group | p.30 | Would shared study broaden participation, and is its conclusion advice or an authorized decision? |
| Petition and member vote | p.34 | What brings a proposal to collective attention, and which participation and decision rules apply? |
| Self-selecting board | p.38 | What makes appointments accountable, and is this arrangement compatible with the co-op's obligations? |

The questions and cautions are our adaptation, not claims that the booklet
endorses each arrangement for incorporated worker co-ops. Founder control,
self-selecting boards, petitions, and juries are comparison material, not
ready-made substitutes for required member or director decisions.

Source limitation: p.22's membership/structure/process text repeats the
consensus pattern despite its initiative-led opening. Do not copy that page
into an executable rule. Clarify the group's actual delegation agreement.

## Separate deliberation, consent, and voting

The circle template describes consent as absence of a serious objection; the
consensus template emphasizes discussing and revising proposals until serious
objections are addressed (pp.14, 18). These are not necessarily enthusiastic
unanimity. Communities use these words differently, so record their definitions.

Ask what counts as an objection, whether standing aside is available, who
facilitates revisions, and whether a fallback vote is authorized. Never invent
a 75–80% fallback because discussion stalled. Distinguish these social processes
from a legally defined written consent resolution; consult the voting reference
and current governing sources for the latter.

The smallest useful decision record states the applicable process, the outcome,
unresolved concerns, the implementation owner, and when to revisit it. This is
a documentation pattern, not a mandatory database schema.

## Design Machines application

For Assembly's 5–50-person co-ops, prefer clarity and shared capacity over more
workflow machinery. Consider the member with limited time, language fluency,
confidence, or access to meetings. Assigning power to whoever has time to do
the work can silence others even when everyone has good intentions.

Trusted first-party Fixture developers remain trusted collaborators. These
community-governance observations do not justify treating developers or Fixtures
as hostile tenants. Nor does a server administrator's technical access itself
establish a community mandate to decide disputes.

Before proposing product work, name the current co-op need, existing feature or
practice it can use, and missing behavior. For discovery and conflict handling,
continue with `discovery-framework.md` and `conflict-systems.md`. Attribute
cultural sources and invite community adaptation rather than presenting a
borrowed practice as a universal technique (booklet p.6).
